## Simple Foreknowledge

#### - David Hunt

### Definition

- (SF) Simple Foreknowledge: God has complete and infallible knowledge of the future.

### Justifications
#### 1. Bible
Is 41:22-23 God knows what will happen before it happens.
Gen 40 God tells Joseph the meaning of dreams that do happen just like it afterwards.
Matt 26 Jesus knows various details about his betrayal and Peters 3x denial that later happen just like it.
Ps 139:40 God knows the words of the Psalmist before he speaks them.
And there are many more ... .

#### 2. God is maximally great

We want to think of God as highly as possible, and attributing (SF) to God makes Him greater than thinking He does not know the future or that He does so only with some additional constrains.

#### 3. God's sovereignty and providence

God's sovereignty and providence require that He has (SF) he got to know the content of His own providential plan for the future. Note, that this does not say anything on *how* he makes the plan or *how* he knows, and definitely not say anything about the criteria of His plan and if He exercises all His power or freely decided to constrain Himself - it simply states *that* He knows. 

#### 4. Church thinker consensus

Most church thinkers across many denominations and across time believe God has (SF), including Justin Matyr, St Augustine, Boethius, St. Anselm, St Thomas Aquinas, Martin Luther, John Calvin, John Wesley, 

#### Falsifiability

All four Justifications are not a proof, but good support. To falsify the (SF) view one would have to show an alternative view that matches better with the four justifications or provide a sound argument against it.

### Failed counter arguments

#### The problem of human freedom

- (1) If God foreknows that Adam will sin, then historically necessarily Adam will sin
- (2) If Adam will sin historically necessarily, then Adam will not sin of his own free will
- ∴ If God foreknows that Adam will sin, then Adam will *not* sin of his own free will
- (3) God foreknows that Adam will sin
- (4) Adam will sin of his own free will

(1), (2), (3) and (4) all seem to be true, but (4) is contradicting ∴ , thus at least one of the statements must be false. Denying that God has (SF) would fix the problem and is the intention of this argument, but that is not the only option.

##### Timeless knowledge

Boethius and Augustine modified (3) and (1) to be 

- (3B) God timelessly knows that Adam will sin.
- (1B) If God timelessly knows that Adam will sin, then Adam will sin

This historical necessity is now gone and therefore the conclusion ∴ no longer follows, but (SF) is preserved.

##### Deterministic free will

Calvin, Luther and prior Augustine modified (4) to be

- (4C) Adam will sin voluntarily, based on his God given desires

That modifies the conclusion ∴ accordingly and makes the argument valid, but it sacrifices libertarian freedom (LF).

##### Retroactive Logical Causation

William Ockham modified (1) to be

- (1aO) If Adam will freely sin, then God will foreknow it
- (1bO) If God foreknows that Adam will sin, then Adam will sin (historically necessarily but not logically necessarily)

In essence, Ockham asserts that logically Adam's sin is the cause of the content of God's foreknowledge, even though temporally, God's foreknowledge comes first. Hunt rejects this view because of time travel paradoxes. However, that seems to assume static time, but reality really operates on dynamic time. Its not clear if Hunt's objections still hold on dynamic time.

##### Foreknown voluntary choice

Augustine modified (2) to be

- (2A) If Adam will sin historically necessarily, then Adam will sin voluntarily

Here Adam is not coerced or manipulated in any way by God's foreknowledge to sin or not to sin, so he does sin voluntarily - even though this voluntary sinning is different from free sinning, since Adam has only one option. Consequently, (LF) libertarian freedom is sacrificed, since Adam can not do otherwise, but even with the only one option he has, since it is voluntarily chosen, Adam is morally accountable.

Ingmar: Seems to me that (LF) can be preserved with this sequence of logical steps:
a) Counterfactual of creaturely freedom logically prior to God's decree: 
		If Adam was in the garden, he would freely sin (LF)
b) God knows a)
c) Divine decree: God puts Adam in the garden
d) Adam is in the garden and voluntarily sins - because of his own nature and free decision as in a) and unaffected by b) but constrained by c) to now decide not freely, but merely voluntarily, and thus still being morally accountable for the choice.

## Middle Knowledge

#### - William Lane Craig

### Molinism Defined

Future states can be described as those that **could be**, **would be**, or **will be**. They are respectively, what is logically possible, what given some condition is the matching outcome, or what will actually happen. All will-be-truths are included in the set of would-be-truths which in turn are a sub set of the could-be-truths: { could be } > { would be } > { will be }. The statements in the { would be } are called **counterfactuals**, they are of the form *if a is true, then b will be true*. The statements in { will be } are those of { would be } where *a* actually is true. { would be } holds those and in addition all for which *a* is actually not true. And finally { could be } holds all of the above and statements, where *b* does not follow form *a*, meaning all the other logically possible alternatives, no matter how unlikely.

Theologians call **{ could be } God's natural knowledge**, since God knows all that could naturally be true. They call **{ will be } God's free knowledge**, since God freely decreed what will be true in our world. And finally, they call **{ would be } God's middle knowledge**, since it is in the middle between the other two in terms of number of truths contained and number of constrains considered. 

A crucial distinction regarding God's middle knowledge is whether God possessed it logically prior or subsequent to decreeing the world. 

- **If subsequent**, then God first decrees how people choose and then, because of His decree, knows how people will decide in each circumstance they will face. This implies that people do not choose freely, but will do exactly as God has chosen for them.

- **If prior**, then God contemplated many different feasible worlds according to all the variations of circumstances he could create for us. And through His middle knowledge He would know all our personal counterfactuals. That means He would know how people would freely chose in any feasible circumstance. And after our decisions are contemplated, He would decree that particular world He deemed best. And apparently He chose a world in which He granted us genuine free will, such that love and trust in Jesus are real decisions we make. And furthermore, a world in which He can use our sinful decisions for our great benefit, culminating in Jesus sacrifice on the cross. He places people just in the right circumstances such that by enlarge He can let them freely chose and still sovereignly achieve His purposes.

The Molinist view is that that God possesses middle knowledge logically prior to decreeing the word, resulting in three logical steps of possible worlds

1. All logical possible worlds, according to God's natural knowledge
2. All feasible worlds according to God's middle knowledge
3. Our world according to God's free knowledge and His decree

### Theological Arguments
#### Divine foreknowledge compatible with future contingents

*Divine Foreknowledge* means that God knows infallibly what will happen in the future. And *Future Contingents* are events or decisions, particularly human decisions to commit sin, in the future that are not deterministic. They could be otherwise. They are not decided nor caused by God. The question is if those two concepts are compatible. They must be compatible, because scripture teaches both. However, this argument calls the compatibility in question:

1. (Necessarily?!) if God foreknows X, then X will happen.
2. (Necessarily??) God foreknows X
3. Therefore, X will (necessarily??) happen.

The argument challenges the compatibility, because from God's foreknowledge in the premises follows logically that the corresponding decision is not free but logically causally determined by God. However, if and where we add the word necessarily is significant. An statement, event or decision is *logically necessary* if they is no other logical alternative. Examples are: God necessarily must exist as the creator of the universe. God necessarily must foreknow all events and decisions because He is omniscient. Thus, the 1. first (necessarily?!) is appropriate and must be there. This is indicated with '?!'. In the 2. second premise, the necessarily is not appropriate, because the argument revolves around future contingents. Thus, we are dealing with events or decisions X that are assumed to be contingent, the opposite of being logically necessary. Adding necessarily to the premise is begging the question. Hence, we got to leave the necessarily out. But without it added to premise 2, it logically also does not belong to the conclusion. This is indicated with '??'. Consequently, the conclusion is that 'X will happen', but not necessarily, which means merely contingently. But that in turn confirms that divine foreknowledge and future contingencies are compatible.

For argument sake, we can allow the second necessarily, which then logically implies the third one as well. But now to make the second premise true, we can no longer have X be a future contingency, but merely some timeless truth, such as 2+2 is 4. So necessarily God knows math and necessarily the solutions to math problems are according to God's knowledge and could not be otherwise. However, that is missing the point of arguing about future contingencies.

Asides from Craig's article, I find the compatibility intuitive, based on my own human knowledge: If I see in the weather forecast that tomorrow there is a snow storm coming with 100% certainty, then foreknow the snow, but clearly snow storms are contingent (in principle it could not snow) and my foreknowing the event is not magically transforming the snow storm into a logically necessary event. Furthermore, my foreknowing it is by no means the cause for the snow storm.

In summary, *Divine Foreknowledge* and *Future Contingents* are compatible, because the Bible teaches both, because its common sense, because there is no direct logical contradiction and because the best attempts of counter arguments fail.

##### The basis of divine foreknowledge of future contingents

This argument tries to deny the possibility of divine foreknowledge based on the non-existence of the future:

1. Only events that actually exist can be known by God.
2. Future events do not actually exist.
3. Therefore, future events cannot be known by God.

This is a logically valid argument, but it is not sound because the premises are not true. The second premise 2. relates to the nature of time, which can be dynamic (God is the conductor in a live orchestra performance) or static (God controls the DVD remote during a DVD-play of the orchestra performance). If time is indeed static as many people believe, especially those that believe in the possibility of time travel, then the second premise is false. But if time is indeed dynamic, as common sense suggest and more careful reflection can proof, then the second premise is true. The proof is detailed elsewhere, but what works for me is that Jesus sacrifice removes our sin guilt, which is only possible if our past selves no longer exist. Thus, on dynamic time Jesus can save us, but on static time He can not. Hence, time must be dynamic. So we will accept 2. as true.

Premise 1. relates to God's future knowledge being *perceptual* or *conceptual*. If perceptual, then God perceives the future through *foresight*, he 'looks into the future and sees or observes what's there'. This view would imply that when there is nothing there then there is nothing to see. So then even God  could not see non-existing or not-yet-existing events. However, then God would not be able to see preset events either if they are not visible or not even bound to anything material, such as ideas in my mind.  No, God's knowledge is conceptual, He simply has the divine property of knowing all past, present and future truths. One could use the ontological argument to show this, as this type of knowledge is a great-making property - God is greater if He inherently knows the future then if He needs to spend effort to look into the future to find out what will be. And since He is the greatest conceivable being, His knowledge must be conceptual. Thus, God can know events without to need to look at them and therefore He can know them without the need of them already existing. It also makes common sense. I can know myself that an anvil will fall straight down, even before it hits the ground. 

One could accept only that God knows future circumstances and future contingents, but the actual outcomes of future decisions. However, the latter follows from the definition of middle knowledge: 

1. God knows how any person will freely decide under any possible circumstance 
2. God knows the exact circumstance any person will be in
3. Therefore, God knows how any person will freely decide

Both premises are true because of God's inherent divine conceptual knowledge. And because of common sense: God should know more than I do, and I already know several examples for each of the two premises. God should know the whole set.

---

In summary, there is no logical compatibility issue between divine foreknowledge and future contingents, God can have conceptual divine foreknowledge as well as conceptual divine knowledge of future contingents even when the future does not yet exist. In combination this amounts to God having middle knowledge being a logically sound view.

#### Divine Providence

Divine providence is how God provides for the world, or more specifically how God made His plan for the world and how he is ensuring that His plan is carried out.

Consider these verses:

> Acts 2:23 
> 23 You have taken Him, who was handed over to you by the ordained counsel and foreknowledge of God, and by lawless hands have crucified and killed Him

> Acts 4:27-28
> 27  Indeed, both Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles and the people of Israel, were assembled together against Your holy Son Jesus whom You have anointed,   28  to do what Your hand and Your counsel had foreordained to be done.

Clearly, the Bible ascribes to the sinful actions of Judas, Herod, Pilate and the Jews that they were part of God's foreknowledge and His foreordination, meaning his eternal divine plan.

With foreknowledge interpreted as **middle knowledge** these verses make a lot of sense: God knew which people under the which circumstances would accuse, sentence and crucify Jesus. So He placed those people in exactly those circumstances and they did what they did freely. Consequently, all blame for sinful actions goes rightly to the people that committed them, but at the same time God's glorious plan to provide a means for our salvation was accomplished. Divine providence and human free decisions proceeded in perfect synergy. And the people involved did not even realize that they were part of this bigger plan:

> 1 Corinthians 2:8
> 8  None of the rulers of this age knew it. For had they known it, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory.

**Open Theism** can not give a good interpretation of these verses in Acts as both, foreknowledge and foreordination require concrete knowledge of the future.

**Augustinian-Calvinism** at least makes logical sense here, as God foreordained and decreed all these events and based on that had the matching foreknowledge. But it also logically forces to ascribe authorship of sin to God, as He was the one who caused Judas to betray Jesus, etc. And on this view the sinners were forced to sin, they had no chance or ability to do otherwise, but nonetheless, God is holding them accountable for their sins. That turns God into the devil.

**Simple Foreknowledge** is also inadequate, as on this view God does not foreordain carefully selected circumstances, but merely preplans how He will react to the decisions of free creatures. He foreknows how a domino will fall and then plans how to setup the circumstances for the next one. But that does not provides a mechanism to orchestrate the synergy of just the right people being at just the right place at just the right time that they would freely betray and crucify Jesus.

**Middle Knowledge** is therefore the only view that can provide a reasonable interpretation of those verses.

### Philosophical Arguments

- Counterfactual of Creaturely Freedom (CCF):  If person P where in circumstance C, P would freely do action A

This CCF definition can be matched to true propositions. One example is, 

- Omelet-CCF: "if I have not eaten in a day and my wife cooks an omelet for me, then I will freely eat it."  

1 Corinthians 2:8 quoted above is another example directly from the Bible. And it seems intuitive that there should be an abundance of other true CCF.

**God has middle knowledge argument:**

1. *If there are true CCF, then God knows these truths.*

This is true because of the definition of God's omniscience: for any proposition x, if x is is true then God knows x and does not believe not-x. Substitute counterfactual for x and it's clear 1. is true by this definition. Craig further explains why any of the uncommon alternative definitions are not valid. 

2. *There are true CCF.*

As the Ometet-CCF and 1 Corinthians 2:8 show, it is intuitively clear that there are at least some true CCF.

The **grounding objection** tries to deny that there could be any true CCF, because they are supposed to be true prior to God's creative decree - a state in which there exists nothing that could make them true and without such ground they can not be true. Apparently this is the most significant or even the only reason why many philosophers reject middle knowledge. Alvin Plantinga's unrefuted response paraphrased: *it is more likely that some CCF are at least possibly true than that CCF must be grounded.* 

A popular view is that the truth of a statement is grounded in its corresponding **truth-maker**, which is something in reality that has the corresponding property. For example "snow is white" has as truth-maker the color of snow in reality. However, this view becomes muddy quickly when we think of statements that are obviously true, but just as obviously cannot have a corresponding truth-maker in reality. For example, "dinosaurs are extinct" is a true statement about the non-existence of dinosaurs and the truth-maker cannot be a dinosaur, nor anything else. So there is none. Somehow this seems to be a problem for philosophers, but seems to me that even just one counter example makes clear we cannot demand that in principle every true statement must have a truth-maker. So since truth can be expressed without, there is no problem with CCF being a class of statements without corresponding truth-makers.

A better view on the grounding of the truth of a statement is the **disquotation principle**, which is that a statement such as "snow is white" is true simply when the state described in the quotation marks is in fact true. So if snow is indeed white then "snow is white" is a true statement. Applied to CCF it means that a CCF such as the Omelet-CCF above is true exactly when in the condition described the consequence described will happen, meaning I would indeed eat the omelet. And nothing in this thought experiment is dependent on the timing of this. It can be in the past, present or future. It is also not dependent on the condition described ever being reality. A a lot of "If I was rich, then ..." CCFs will never become reality, but nonetheless they could be true statements. Therefore, the disquotation principle is a viable option for the grounding of the truth of a CCF - or alternatively we can simply view CCF as statements that can be true without the need for any grounding. Either way we arrive at the confirmation of premise 2., that there are true CCF.

3. *If God knows true CCF, then God knows them* 
   *either logically prior to* 
   *or logically posterior to the divine creative decree.*

This must be true, since logically prior or posterior are the only possible options and logical opposites of each other, making the statement of the form "if x then a or not a", which is always true. 

4. *CCF cannot be known only logically posterior to the divine creative decree*

We concluded above that true CCF are either grounded in the disquotation principle or can be true without the need for any grounding. In the analysis for both views we saw that they are timeless and thus their truth is not affected by being evaluated before or after the divine creative decree. Therefore their knowability is also the same before and after. Hence, God can know true CCF both, prior and posterior to the divine creative decree.

However, if we assume that God could know true CCF only after his divine creative decree, as is the Augustinian-Calvinist view, then it is actually God who determines what every person does in any actually obtaining circumstance. The person will then not freely do action A, but will do A according to his compatibilistic freedom which invalidates our definition of CCF and thus invalidates our assumption at the start of this paragraph. Therefore, true CCF in fact must be known by God prior to his divine decree.

That concludes all premises and their defenses. Now purely based on logical rules:

From (1.) and (2.) follows

5. *Therefore, God knows true CCF* 

From (3.) and (5.) follows

6. *Therefore, God knows true CCF* 
   *either logically prior to* 
   *or logically posterior to the divine creative decree.*

From (4.) and (6.) follows

7. *Therefore, God knows true CCF* 
   *logically prior to the divine creative decree.*

That (7.) is the essence of divine middle knowledge, which is to say this is a logically sound argument for God having middle knowledge.

### Conclusion

Just as the trinity, God's middle knowledge is not explicitly taught by the biblical text, but is consistent with scripture which same of the other views are not.  Theologically it is compelling since it is better able that the other three views to tie together the basis and compatibility of divine foreknowledge and future contingents as well as God's sovereign providence over a world of genuinely free creature. Philosophically, there is a logically sound argument for God having middle knowledge. Hence, we should affirm the middle knowledge view.

## Rebuttals

#### - Gregory A. Boyd

### Future openness
Scripture includes accounts of God changing His mind and feeling regret, surprise and disappointment. Molinism has to interpret those as anthropomorphic which is too much of a stretch to be acceptable.

### Grounding of CCF

According to Molinism, all true CCF are truths that are eternally settled before they take place. But they are 

- not determined by God as that would make them not free
- not by the creature as that would mean retroactive causation
- so must be just a brute fact co-existing eternally with but independent of God.

That is counterintuitive at best, challenging God's unique self existence at worst.

### Libertarian freedom

- Libertarian Freedom (LF):  If person P where in circumstance C, P could freely choose action A or not A

How can an agent have LF if he does not determine his own actions, but they have been determined for him since eternity past decided so by ... nothing? To possess LF an agent must himself be the one that resolves indefinite possibilities into settled facts.

### Would Counterfactuals and Might Counterfactuals

In addition to the three types of God's knowledge,  { could be } > { would be } > { will be }, we should attribute to God a fourth kind of the set of decisions that might be, which fit between could and would be. They are the type of counterfactuals that are the constraint options that a free agent is choosing between in any set of circumstance he's in. 

- Might-Counterfactual:  If person P where in circumstance C, P might freely do one of the actions { A, B, C, .... }.

The sets of truths known by God becomes:  { could be } > { might be } > { would be } > { will be }.

This view preserves the advantages of could-counterfactuals middle knowledge and adds

- Genuine Libertarian Freedom (LF)
- Creaturely decisions are not settled until the creature make the decision and are thus grounded in the creaturely will
- Verses about God changing His mind and feeling regret, surprise and disappointment can be taken at face value.

### Foreknowledge and Fatalism

Fatalism:

1\*. Necessarily if was true that X will happen, then X will happen.
2\*. Necessarily it was true that X will happen
3. Therefore, X will necessarily happen.

(2*) is indeed not true as Craig explained.

Divine Foreknowledge:

1#. Necessarily if God foreknew X, then X will happen.
2#. Necessarily God foreknew X

3. Therefore, X will necessarily happen.

(2#) is true, because of temporal necessity. Just because there is no adequate theory translating temporal necessity to logical necessity does not mean logical necessity does not apply here.

### Expressive power of middle knowledge 

Its indeed great for explaining the synergy between divine control and creaturely freedom.

Its not so great for the problem of evil, where a loving and all powerful God of middle knowledge created people that will reject Christ even though God knew they would. Why did God not simply refrain from creating those people? The simple foreknowledge view does not have this problem, since God could not have refrained from creating those people. And the open theism neither has this problem because there God did not even know who those people are.

### True Counterfactuals of Creaturely Freedom (CCF) exist?

- J: If Jones is offered the job, he will accept it.
- J~: If Jones is offered the job, he will *not* accept it.

Libertarian freedom posits that both, J and J~ should be equally possible to be true in the same world under the same circumstances. But the truth of J and J~ is mutually exclusive. Thus, neither is true, and instead we should have the actually true statement

- J\*: If Jones is offered the job, he *might or might not* accept it.

Thus, there are no CCF as defined by Craig and therefore God does not have middle knowledge.

## Reflections

#### - Ingmar Bitter

### Retroactive  Causation

Boyd rejects retroactive causation and justifies it by claiming Craig rejects it, too. However, Craig did not do so in this article and I am not aware of him doing so elsewhere either. It seems that causation that is retroactive in time can still be viewed as forward causation logically, which is the more important axis in this discussion anyways. It will address the grounding objection as given by Boyd, since the truth of CCF is then grounded in the will of the free creature. And then, logically posterior to the creatures decision, God foreknows the decision and it becomes part of His middle knowledge. It will also answer Hunt's issue of J and J~ being mutually exclusive, since in logical order, Jones is first presented with the job, he then decides to accept it or not and logically posterior but temporally prior to that God's foreknows that decision. Thus only one of J and J~ are part of God's foreknowledge, the logical contradiction is removed and it is again valid to attribute middle knowledge to God. And thirdly, it makes it even more clear why divine foreknowledge and CCF are compatible, thus satisfying Helm's concerns. 

- Libertarian Freedom (LF):  If person P where in circumstance C, P *could* freely choose action A or not A
- Counterfactual of Creaturely Freedom (CCF):  If person P where in circumstance C, P *would* freely choose A

Boyd and Hunt seems to think that LF and CCF are not compatible. But they are. Prior to P choosing A or not A, both options are equally valid. Posterior to choosing the decision is settled and becomes a CCF. God's middle knowledge is made up of those posterior CCF. And his natural knowledge of the prior LF. And since it is the creature himself that causes the transition from LF to CCF there is on restriction on his freedom.

### The problem of evil

Clearly, some specific instances of evil are needed for God's bigger picture plan and glory. For example, the sinful rebellion of Pharaoh against God was needed so God could display His superior power. Judas sinful betrayal of Jesus was needed so that Jesus would be captured and crucified - without which we would have no means of salvation. And thirdly, there are many accounts of sinful actions in the Bible that are paired with the associated worldly and divine consequences. Those all serve now as means for teaching and wisdom to all Christians. They therefore have a higher purpose and benefit that outweighs the harm they did at the time.  We are not capable to quantify the impact of all other evil and good in the past, present and future of our world that we would be able to measure the cost/benefit ratio of all evil that God did allow vs all good that is achieved here and will be by us later in in heaven when we praise and serve God there for eternity. Therefore we cannot reject divine foreknowledge by claiming that there is too much evil in this world. God may very well have morally acceptable reasons for each one of them.

### Future openness
If we assume that God has morally acceptable reasons to allow some evil in the world, then it is still consistent with His character to not be happy about it. So he can have all the emotions such as anger, grief, sadness, etc.   about some evil that any upright Christian would have as well. 

In Isaiah 5:1-7, God is comparing the Israelites to a vineyard that in spite of caring preparation brought forward bad fruit unexpectedly. Since God is the one expecting otherwise, this could be sees as God being surprised and thus having failed to have foreknowledge. But the context also warrants to see unexpected as inconsistent with the intended outcome. That is not a stretch in interpretation and makes sense in the purpose of the passage to teach that God does care for us but but will not be happy with us disobeying Him and will remove His protection form us as a consequence. Communicating that of course has the intend that we will learn from the mistakes of the Israelites and not repeat them ourselves.

In Jeremiah 18:7-10 describes alternate futures that Boyd interprets as God changing His mind. But there is no such thing. The passage is actually a good example of future contingencies. God lays out how He himself will act in different possible circumstances. He is giving Israel the freedom which of those circumstances to create. A true change of mind would mean that in the same circumstance God first reacts one way and then for the same circumstance reacts another way. That is not the case here, nor anywhere else.

Therefore, for none of the so-called examples of God's regret, surprise and disappointment is there a need to interpret those as anthropomorphic. They are either appropriate as literal or misinterpreted by Boyd.

### Necessary Foreknowledge

Hunt claimed contrary to Craig that this argument is sound:

1#. Necessarily if God foreknew X, then X will happen.
2#. Necessarily God foreknew X

3. Therefore, X will necessarily happen.

Craig rejected 2# on the observation that logical necessity does not follow from temporal necessity. And Hunt thinks that it does not fail to follow, we merely do not know how to show it. But a single counter example should suffice to show a general rule is wrong. So as long as X is a contingent event, such as "it's snowing" it is clear that the event is not necessary and can therefore not be necessarily foreknown. Therefore, 2# is indeed false.



## The Augustinian-Calvinist View

#### - Paul Helm

> Ingmar: Helm frequently uses the terms compatibilist and in-compatibilist when referring to proponents of deterministic freedom and libertarian freedom, respectively. Since the debate of the book is about which views are compatible logically, with omniscience, foreknowledge, causation, freedom, accountability and the Bible as a whole - and since the authors differ on what's compatible and what's not, I'll not use Helm's labels, but instead deterministic freedom and libertarian freedom.

### Divine Omniscience and Human Freedom - logical compatibility

Divine omniscience and human freedom are logically compatible, if the latter is defined as 

* Libertarian Freedom (LF):  
  	If person P where in circumstance C, 
    		P could freely choose action A or not-A

This stands in contrast to 

* Deterministic Freedom (DF): 
  	When person P will be in circumstance C, 
    		P can only choose action A

LF has no theological problems, but DF unmodified has as logical conclusion that person P can not be held morally accountable for his sinful actions. The typical way out is to adopt a reduced account of divine omniscience such that moral accountability becomes logically compatible.  However, Helm will argue instead for a different definition of DF and does not require a reduced view of divine omniscience. 

#### Divine Foreknowledge

1. Straight Causation: God's knowledge of the future is the cause of things. 
   Logically incoherent exception: God's knows future sins but does not cause them, He merely permits them. - Thomas Aquinas
2. Post-decree: Logically, God first decrees what will happen, then He has knowledge of what the details of his decree is and then as time unfolds, things do happen according to His decree and foreknowledge.
3. Pre-decree: Logically, God first foreknows the future and then decrees this or that conditioned on His foreknowledge, and then as time unfolds, things do happen according to His decree and foreknowledge.

Helm's view works with definitions 1. and 2. but not with 3.

#### Scriptural boundaries 

Whatever view we hold, it must affirm that "everything that is worth God's notice is governed by Him", making his providence "universal and yet particular". But also God ordains everything that comes to pass, even evil and sinful actions, as long as they were under the agents control. For those sinful actions, scripture teaches that we are accountable to God and that in judging us God takes into consideration our thoughts and intentions of our hearts as well as the state of or relative ignorance or knowledge of His applicable commands.

Since both are scriptural, divine sovereignty and human accountability must be logically compatible, even though it is not obvious why that is so.

The strongest example is Acts 2:23 that tells us that,  *Jesus according to God's plan and foreknowledge was handed over and crucified by the Jews and those outside the law.*  This affirms the sinful action as part of God's plan as well as the actors accountability before God for their sins.

Since the universe is all there is apart form God, there can only be one universe. Plato had issues with that conclusion, since we have no observational experience the creation of universes. But the conclusion still holds. However, if one was to conclude that those that crucified Jesus were puppets of God because we have no observational experience to the contrary, then that is not a valid analogy and we must not believe they were puppets. It is better to say in this case, that causality is incomprehensible since there is nothing like it in human experience. But although the full picture is incomprehensible, parts can be understood.

### Argument 1: Foreknowledge, Freedom and Divine Grace

Faith in Christ is the instrumental cause of salvation.

On libertarian freedom, God's grace is casually necessary, but not casually sufficient.

On Helm's deterministic freedom view, God's grace is both, casually necessary and casually sufficient. And receiving God's grace is liberating as it frees the saved person from the bondage to sin. In addition, grace changes the state of mind from not being able to want God to being able to and ensuring that we do want God. The whole premise of libertarian freedom regarding choosing to accept grace is ill-founded. Man cannot want to accept grace, thus only a deterministic freedom view has any chance of anyone getting saved. God must determine for someone to be freed from bondage to sin and to be made spiritually alive and able to want a relationship with God.

#### Argument 2: Divine Perfection and Providence

God's omniscience should be understood as widely as possible. Thus he should know even the future free actions of free agents.

This is assumed by the middle knowledge view that sees God as knowing all possible worlds pre-decree and then choosing one to actualize, but in doing so that actual world, our world, will play out exactly as it must, so all so-called free decisions are actually fully determined - can they therefore still be called free? Can sin not be attributed to God? No - thus making Molinism unacceptable.

In the Augustinian view, God cannot govern sin actions, but He can and does infallibly know of their occurrences. God knows the events He brings about and also those he does not bring about. God does not cause sin actions, but *willingly permits* them. This entails that God withholds his steadying hand when humans commit sins as long as in the overall picture His good will is still accomplished. Therefore He can not be labeled as the author of sin. However, it does not explain why humans, created by a good God only do evil when left on their own. But the other three views do not have a good answer for that either.

So God governs all actions except of the sinful ones. Nonetheless, Helm thinks that typing his essay, presumably not sinful, was not governed by God but was his own action. Helm also quotes William Hasker, according to whom Calvinism entails that God causes everything that happens without exception. Helm sees Calvinism different in making exceptions for all sin actions.  

#### Argument 3: The incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom

Assume X == Jones will freely eat a tuna sandwich tomorrow.

1. Logically necessarily, if God foreknows X, then X will happen.
2. Historically necessarily, God foreknows X
3. Therefore, X will historically necessarily happen.

But then X cannot be a free act such as choosing which sandwich to eat. Consequently, divine foreknowledge is incompatible with libertarian freedom.

## Rebuttals

## - Gregory A. Boyd

### Argument 3: The incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom

Boyd thinks argument 3 is fine. Boyd fixes the incompatibility by modifying what God knows, and Helm by modifying libertarian freedom to deterministic freedom.

#### Anthropomorphic and symbolic openness verses

Verses that attribute surprise and change of mind to God are dismissed by Helm as Anthropomorphic and/or symbolic. Boyd counters that even if they are, then there is still truth that is intended to be communicated, and what other truth could match than that in God's mind the future is not exhaustively settled, but for the outcomes of some free decisions God knows multiple possibilities and may be surprised at the actual one when it is being decided - but not unprepared.

#### Condition vs cause

Faith in Christ is the *cause* of salvation as stated by Helm is confusing *condition* with *cause*. Meeting the condition of willingly accepting a gift in no way makes the gift earned.  Man being able to seek and trust God without any drawing by God is a straw man idea not really believed by anyone in the evangelical community. 

## David Hunt

### Scriptural Boundaries

Helm thinks that deterministic freedom (DF) and moral accountability are compatible, but they are not and his arguments are not convincing.

#### Argument 1: Foreknowledge, Freedom and Divine Grace

Hunt finds that grace is not relevant to the compatibility between divine foreknowledge and human freedom. He agrees that grace is needed as solution to our accountability to God. But he disagrees that the Bible teaches that we cannot want to want God. In fact its the opposite, since we are designed by God to want a relationship with Him. Hence, DF is not the correct view, let alone the only option.

#### Argument 2: Divine Perfection and Providence

1. It is theologically desirable that God foreknows free decisions
2. If compatibilism is true, God can foreknow free decisions
3. ∴ compatibilism is true

The two premises are true, but the conclusion ∴ does not follow. It would follow with this second premise

​	2'. If compatibilism is NOT true, God can NOT foreknow free decisions

However Helm does not try to argue for 2'.

#### Argument 3: The incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom

The argument for incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom is not compelling. See Hunt's main essay. Helm's view of the incompatibility is actually not from Augustine nor from Calvin, but from Jonathan Edwards. The other two have a view more like Hunt.

## William Lane Craig

### Scriptural Boundaries

The reason Helm thinks that sovereignty and human accountability are not obviously compatible is because he assumes that human actions are not chosen out of libertarian free will. With that assumption reversed the two are obviously logically compatible and scripture does teach libertarian freedom. See for example 1 Cor 10:13, which states that there is a divinely provided proper choice option for every temptation. Thus, sin must be the agent choosing against the divinely provided option - that is libertarian freedom.

There is on reason to retreat to incomprehensibility because of absence of matching human experience. Especially regarding committing sins, consider an FBI setup providing the suspect with an opportunity to sell drugs to catch him in the act when committing the crime. There the FBI controls all circumstances, but the suspect is still free to choose not to commit the crime.

#### Argument 1: Foreknowledge, Freedom and Divine Grace

Craig finds that grace is not relevant to the compatibility between divine foreknowledge and human freedom.

#### Argument 2: Divine Perfection and Providence

Helm shows that God must foreknow future contingents. This truth is "most at home" with deterministic freedom. Why is that? Helm is probably assuming foreknowledge comes from foreordination? Why not from simple foreknowledge?

God can have foreknowledge of sins, but not be their author. Supposedly, this is not possible on Molinism, but Helm fails to provide an actual argument for that claim. He poses a rhetorical question, on how God could do it, given Molinism. Implying that God cannot, he dismissed Molinism. However, is is easy to answer. If God wants to actualize a particular possible world, all He has to do is create the world with just those circumstances as are defined in the chosen possible world.

On Helm's view, God is not the author of sins He foreknows, because He merely permits them. Fine, but how does He foreknow them? His foreknowledge is supposed to be post-decree, and not causal, then there must be secondary causes that entail the sins. And God knows and causes those secondary causes. That is more complicated an explanation than simple pre-decree foreknowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. Further, it either means that God is not in full control of everything in the universe, which is contrary to typical Augustinian and Calvinist teaching, or God is indirectly responsible for sin, and therefore ultimately the author of sin. Willful permission of sin is only leaving God morally perfect, if the permitted sin is authored by a libertarian free agent.

#### Argument 3: The incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom

Recap: *Divine foreknowledge makes free actions temporally necessary and therefore not free. Further, if Jones was to eat a different sandwich, then Jones would have the power to falsify a belief of God.* Both statements are faulty. Temporal necessary does not imply logical necessary. And in logical arguments we are concerned only about logical necessary. And if Jones is looking at two sandwiches, he can truly chose either one. If its not the tuna sandwich, then he's not falsifying God's believe, but then God would have known this all along and would have never believed in a tuna sandwich. So there is no logical problem here in principle nor in the tuna example.

## Reflections

#### - Ingmar Bitter

### Argument 1: Foreknowledge, Freedom and Divine Grace

Its a good approach to say that *the libertarian freedom (LF) premise may be logically ok, but is theologically impossible, since with LF we could never choose any other than rejecting Jesus, but scripture teaches some do choose Jesus. Hence, deterministic freedom (DF) is the only option*.  Unfortunately, Helm does not backup his claim that this is what scripture teaches. It is true that no one comes to Jesus except those that the Father draws, but that does not mean that all that the Father draws come to Jesus. It allows for some to reject the drawing. The same holds for all other verses that supposedly teach irresistible grace. Therefore, DF is not the only option. And if we can show from scripture that grace is resistible, then DF becomes inconsistent with the Bible. Luke 13:34 is one such counter DF example, since Jesus wanted to gather the people of Jerusalem, but they were not willing.

#### Argument 2: Divine Perfection and Providence

God causes everything but sin. Sin he knowingly permits. So He knows about it before the sin is committed, yet the authorship lies still with the sinning agent. If that is so for Helm's view, then it is still true when evaluating Molinism. Hence, Helms objections to Molinism either invalidate his own view, or as expressed if his essay, invalidate his complains about Molinism.

#### Argument 3: The incompatibility of divine foreknowledge and human freedom

Here we have another example of not understanding retroactive causation. The logical ordering of causes does not have to be forward in time. *If God knows the future, then He can can take that knowledge into consideration when making His plan*. Stated this loosely it makes intuitive sense, but could be interpreted as circular reasoning - what was first, the free decision that caused the plan, or the plan that caused the circumstances leading to the free decision? To be more precise and not circular, we should say that *If God knows Counterfactuals of Creaturely Freedom, then He can can take that knowledge into consideration when making His plan*. Now clearly the authorship of free decisions is with the free creature.
