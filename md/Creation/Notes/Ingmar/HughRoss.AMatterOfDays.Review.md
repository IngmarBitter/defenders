### A Matter of Days, by  Hugh Ross, PhD

### Reviewed by Ingmar Bitter, PhD

##### Review of the 2nd Edition, 2015

Quotations in this review are not word for word, but summaries that retain the key ideas.

Quotations give the idea of the content. Lots of the content I agreed with before reading other content I agreed with after reading it here in this book. What is interesting for discussion are unresolved disagreements. My commentary is restricted to just these few disagreements and are intended solely as constructive criticism, not as personal offense. 

### Foreword

#### by Walter Kaiser, President Emeritus, Cordon-Conwell Theological Seminary

> The book is written with grace, humility, and excellence in Biblical scholarship and scientific research. 
>
> The meaning of the creation days in Genesis has divided Christians and impedes the sharing of the gospel. Christians should be loving to each other, not fight over this issue. Reconciliation has been attempted by several committees by either affirming that multiple interpretations are equally valid to hold, or by affirming that the old age view is correct. But still the controversy remains. 
>
> This book brings another option for reconciliation through careful analysis and 18 scientific predictions that can show which of day-age or 24h is correct. 

If Christians should not fight over the creation day meaning, then consequently a whole book written on the issue that insists that only one view is correct is pouring gasoline on the flames. So by his own standards Kaiser should recommend against this book. 

If the controversy is over science being subordinate to the Bible or not then how can a scientific prediction test resolve the issue? If the test confirms that the day-age view resonates better with science, then reconciliation is achieved through those that say science can not overrule the Bible having to accept that science just overruled the Bible?  I doubt that will be a successful approach.

### Introduction

> Ross has passion for God's Word (the Bible) and for God's creation (science) and finds the opposition to his interpretation of both disheartening, especially since the stronger opposition comes from Christians. Reasons to Believe (RtB) was founded for people that struggle with the young earth creationism (YEC) view. The Bible affirms nature and vice versa.
>
> Reconciliation can be achieved through scientific predictions of competing views and checking which ones are fulfilled. "Peace could reign in just a matter of days."

Dr Jonathan Safarti  wrote [a detailed review of the introduction](http://creation.com/critique-of-intro-chapter-of-hugh-ross-new-book-a-matter-of-days) suggesting that disheartening opposition regularly experienced by YEC as well. Both sides should get better at being "gentle and respectful" (1 Peter 3:15) and both sides need to be willing to listen to constructive criticism of their views. This includes being able to express what one feels is net right with the other view. If the issue of concern is viewed as a *compromise* between competing principles then the use of such a word as label must be possible to be used in a critique - that's not name calling but problem describing. Words mentioned by Ross that were more harsh are net actually part of anything Safarti can remember.

As noted about the foreword, having a scientific test about the authority of science over scripture is a quite biased method. If the tests point to the old age creationism (OEC) position of RtB, then it seems like the result of an unfair setup. However, if the tests conclude that the YEC position is the better scientific interpretation, then I suppose reconciliation can indeed be achieved after all. 

And for laughs, think a little more about the concluding sentence *"Peace could reign in just a matter of days."* If OEC wins the prediction tests then the creation days are millions of years long and thus peace will not be achieved for a long long time. Hence, for anyone hoping for peace, according to Ross's own words should hope the OEC view is false. And if YEC wins, then the days are 24h each and peace will reign really soon, in a matter of days, or maybe even in just one day!

### Chapter 4 Wisdom of the ages - Church fathers' views

> The opinion of the church fathers is important and claimed by YEC to consistently have been 24h creation days.

The YEC claim might be true for some YEC leaders. But it is not the claim that is relevant to the OEC position. The more typical YEC claim is relevant, and that is that *none of the church fathers taught the creation days were long periods of time (>10,000 years)*. If that claim stands than OEC has practically no support from the church fathers. Some church fathers believing in either instantaneous creation or in figurative meaning does deviate from the 24h view, but does not in any way add credibility to a long ages view.

> Philo 13BC-~47AD: instantaneous creation, metaphorical days
>
> Josephus 37-100AD: no quote, "one day" difficult to interpret

Here is what Josephus said in his writing headed **Containing the interval of 3833 years From the Creation to the death of Isaac** :"In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. [...] God commanded that there should be light [...] he [...] separated the light and the darkness; and the name he gave to one was Night, and the other he called day: and he named the beginning of light, and the time of rest, The Evening and The Morning, and this was indeed **the first day**. But *Moses said it was **one day**; the cause of which I am able to give even now; but because I have promised to give such reasons for all things in a treatise by itself, I shall put off its exposition till that time.* After this, on the **second day**, he placed the heaven over the whole world [...]. On the **third day** he appointed the dry land to appear, with the sea itself round about it; and on the very same day he made the plants and the seeds to spring out of the earth. On the **fourth day** he adorned the heaven with the sun, the moon, and the other stars [...]. And on the **fifth day** he produced the living creatures [...] that swim, and [...] fly [...]. On the **sixth day** he created [...] beasts, and [...] man. Accordingly Moses says, that **in just six days the world, and all that is therein, was made**. And that the **seventh day was a rest**, and a release from the labor of such operations; whence it is that we celebrate a rest from our labors on that day, and **call it the Sabbath**, which word denotes **rest in the Hebrew** tongue"

The part in *italics* is the basis for Ross's comment that he wonders if Josephus found one day difficult to interpret. That is assuming reasoning about the absence of the promised explanation. Maybe the explanation was very easy, but its record got lost?  We should not speculate either way.  However, what is clear from the remainder of the quote is that Josephus thought of the creation days as "just six days" and the day of rest being the reason for the Sabbath day. Thus, he saw all 7 creation week as 24h days.  Why is Ross telling us speculations that remotely support his view and not telling us the obvious facts that do not support his view? Even more concerning, Safarti provided the full quote and similar conclusions to mine in his book *Refuting Compromise*, and 11 years after its printing, Ross released a second edition of *A Matter Of Days*. Did he correct this misleading section on Josephus? Well the clearly thought about the section, as the wording has changed from "His comments suggest that" to "One might wonder whether", but the remainder remained, no quote was added and Josephus's clear 24h view was still not mentioned. So available constructive criticism was ignored and instead the misleading narrative was maintained.  I'm disappointed.

> Justin Martyr, 100-165AD: at least one creation could have been 1000 years (Psa 90:4, 2 Pet 3:8)
>
> Irenaeus, ~130-~201AD: same view as Justin Martyr, quote added "In the day that they [Adam and Eve] did eat, in the same did they die ..."

Irenaeus's quote is a little confusing to read - Ross agrees - but the one clear thing about it is what Ross apparently missed: That is is not talking about creation days, but about the fall. There is another quote from Irenaeus that is much clearer: *"For in six days as the world was made, in so many thousand years shall it be concluded. … For that day of the Lord is a thousand years; and in six days created things were completed: it is evident, therefore, that they will come to an end at the sixth thousand year."*  So Irenaeus clearly believed in literal 24h creation days that had a second layer symbolic meaning of being days-as-types-of-millennia identifying the age of universe as six thousand years from creation till destruction by God.

And the omitted quote from Justin Martyr shown the same line of thinking, e.g. that he attached the thousand year idea to the day of the fall and not to a creation day.

Again, this is a quite misleading commentary by Ross. And again this criticism was in Safarti's book for 11 years and did not lead to a correction in the second edition of Ross's book although again the wording of the section was changed so its contend had been consciously revised. I was hoping to find either a refutation of Safarti's comments or a non-misleading restatement of the views of these two church fathers. I'm disappointed.

> Hippolytus 170-235AD: humans were on earth a few thousand years

OK

> Clement of Alexandria 150-~213AD: days are not 24h, but to provide an ordering of events

Clement of Alexandria, Origen and Augustine formed the *School of Alexandria* that tried like Philo before to harmonize Greek philosophy with the Bible, resulting in allegorizing large parts of the Bible, much more than both YEC and OEC can accept. Note that the allegorical interpretation of the days come only from this school of thought, so it should not be thought of as individually contemplated conclusions.

> Origen 185-254AD: creation days have spiritual meaning, days did not exist until day 4 when the sun was made. The 7th day is still ongoing, because of this quote "... the day of the Sabbath and rest of God, which follows ... creation and which lasts during the duration of the world" 

Part of the *School of Alexandria*. The quote is not really supporting Ross's claim, since *follows* and *lasts* refer to a single object, thus the word *which* does not refer to *the Sabbath day **and** rest of God* (two things, plural), but only to the *rest of God* (one thing, singular). Consequently the rest of God continues while the Sabbath day does not. 

Elsewhere Origen clearly stated that he believes in a young earth: *"the Mosaic account of the creation, which teaches that the world is not yet ten thousand years old, but very much under that"*

> Augustine 354-430: creation days were figurative, the 7th day is continuing

Augustine based his writing on the Latin translation of the Greek translation of the original. He believed the earth was less than 6000 years old: from his *Of the Falseness of the History Which Allots Many Thousand Years to the World’s Past* : "reckoning by the sacred writings, we find that not 6,000 years have yet passed."

> Basil 329-379: several quotes no commentary

Ross left out some key parts in his quote. Here is the full [quote](http://www.newadvent.org/fathers/32012.htm) with the missing part <u>underlined</u> and **bold** for the section that makes clear Basil believed in 24h creation days:

> <u>Thus under the form of history the law is laid down for what is to follow. And the evening and the morning were one day.</u> Why does Scripture say one day the first day? <u>Before speaking to us of the second, the third, and the fourth days, would it not have been more natural to call that one the first which began the series? If it therefore says one day, it is from a wish to determine the measure of day and night, and to combine the time that they contain. **Now twenty-four hours fill up the space of one day**— we mean of a day and of a night; and if, at the time of the solstices, they have not both an equal length, the time marked by Scripture does not the less circumscribe their duration. It is as though it said: **twenty-four hours measure the space of a day**, or that, in reality a day is the time that the heavens starting from one point take to return there. Thus, every time that, in the revolution of the sun, evening and morning occupy the world, their periodical succession never exceeds the space of one day. But must we believe in a mysterious reason for this? God who made the nature of time measured it out and determined it by intervals of days; and, wishing to give it a week as a measure, he ordered the week to revolve from period to period upon itself, to count the movement of time, forming the week of one day revolving seven times upon itself: a proper circle begins and ends with itself. Such is also the character of eternity, to revolve upon itself and to end nowhere.</u> If then the beginning of time is called one day rather than the first day, it is because Scripture wishes to establish its relationship with eternity. It was, in reality, fit and natural to call one the day whose character is to be one wholly separated and isolated from all the others. If Scripture speaks to us of many ages, saying everywhere, age of age, and ages of ages, we do not see it enumerate them as first, second, and third. It follows that we are hereby shown not so much limits, ends and succession of ages, as distinctions between various states and modes of action.

Hence Basil should count as YEC church father.

> Ambrose of Milan 339-397AD: cited often by YEC, quotes of Ambrose commenting on different uses of day in the Bible, but not clear what he believed about the age of the universe

Why is it not clear? The very quotes provided start with "Scripture established the law that twenty-four hours ... should be given the name of day ...".  That's very clear. And the latter "scripture appeals at times at an age of the world" describes an exception to the law that needs to be justified and his examples for such are not from the creation days.  According to Safarti, Ross himself has elsewhere stated “He appears to imply, though, that the creation days are twenty-four hour periods”. Why is Ross not telling us that in his book?

Loving tolerance should also be the mode of today, especially between Christians. And in most cases I think that actually is the case. ### Chapter 6 Toward better Interpretations - scientific method applied to hermeneutics

> From the Bible: God does not and cannot lie, his Word is flawless, perfect, right, true, unbreakable. The heavens, sky and creation display knowledge, proclaim Gods glory, eternal power and divine nature.

Good stuff!

> Language barriers and human limitation con lead to misunderstandings. Willingness to recognize and work though obstacles brings unity. 

I totally agree. I hope in the future Ross will follow this directive better than he did in this second edition - as I pointed out before that he did not make changes to misleading passages although critique of it was available in print for over a decade.

> Bible translation is difficult because words have differing semantic ranges. Many different parallel translations are required to convey the proper meaning.

I agree on the semantic range problem when considered word for word. But from the context the particular intended meaning can be determined and then that meaning can be accurately translated in a single translation - especially when the target language has many more words than the source as it is the case for English.

> Hebrew has only one word for a long period of time: yom

That is not correct, if God wanted to express long ages with a chorus like "the first long time", "the second long time", ... then he could have used a number with
[h5769](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lang/lexicon/lexicon.cfm?Strongs=H5769&t=KJV) עוֹלָם `owlam: long duration, antiquity; and
[h6256](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lang/lexicon/lexicon.cfm?Strongs=H6256&t=KJV) עֵת `eth: time, occurrence. Both are used elsewhere in the OT.

> Human limitations include limited knowledge, bias based on background and assumptions.  Preperly identifying literary genre (historical, bibliographical, poetry, stylized narrative, apolyptical, mythical, ...) and layers of meaning (plain, analogy, motaphor, ...) 

Agreed.

> the scientific method applied to textual understanding can reduce error due to human limitations. It is comprised of these steps:
>
> 1. Identify relevant texts and observations
> 2. Identify their frames of reference
> 3. Identify their contexts and and initial conditions
> 4. Identify the action: What? Where? When? In which order? 
> 5. Identify the final conditions
> 6. Make a tentative interpretation explaining the How and Why
> 7. Review (6) in light of additional relevant texts and missed information
> 8. Revise (6) based on (7)
> 9. Restart at (1) and improve

This is a  method that indeed will help not drawing conclusions too early. However, steps (7) and (8) are not necessary if we are thorough in (1). And if everything was thorough up to (8) then 9 is net needed either - unless with time new information becomes available (this may included missed information). A more concise list of steps is:

1. Identify relevant texts and observations
2. Identify their frames of reference
3. Identify their contexts and and initial conditions
4. Identify the action: What? Where? When? In which order? 
5. Identify the final conditions
6. Make a tentative interpretation explaining the How and Why
7. Restart at (1) when additional (or missed) information becomes available

> Genesis 1,2 interpretations should include information from all other Biblical texts on creation, incl. Gen 1-11; Job 9, 34-41; Psa 104, 139, 147-148; Pro 8; Ecc 1, 3, 8-12; Isa 40-51; Rom 1-8; 1 Cor 15; 2 Cor 4; Heb 1, 4; 2 Peter 3; Rev 20-22 (from table on p.59)

Agreed.

> Interpretations should be tested by how well their predictions are fulfilled. Failed predictions should be added as new information of step (7) and then the process to make an improved interpretation is restarted at step (1). 

OK.

> Eliminating inquiry and discussion by allowing only one view or declaring all views as true leads to frustration and conflicts. Each interpretation should be fair game for testing.

I totally agree. However, anyone should be allowed to define what kind of testing is done. The more diverse a set of tests the better. A conclusion should only be drawn when all competing view proponents had a chance to see their suggested tests evaluated.

### Chapter 7 - Anchored in Scripture - Scriptural support for looong days

> **יוֹם  yom** = a) some part of daylight time  b) sunrise to sunset  c) sunset to sunset (24h) and  d) a time period or age with definite beginning and end  e) a year
>
> The context "the day of the Lord" is a time span of a month or longer, but depends on the interpretation of yet to be fulfilled prophecy.

Option e) a year was not in Ross's list but should have been - so I added it.

Hard to tell what exactly Ross is referring to regarding *the day of the Lord*. Probably to Zechariah 14:7-9, since this [article](http://www.oldearth.org/word_study_yom.htm) that is quite similar to this chapter explicitly refers to it: *"the 'one day' refers to a period of time when the Lord shall be king over the earth."* But that is a very strange view. The most complicated timing concept in his article is that the phrase probably does not each time refer to the same day. Some refer to a day that already passed, other to a day yet to come. The root word עֶ֥רֶב occurs only [18x](http://biblehub.com/hebrew/erev_6153.htm), 17x it means *evening* (singular) and once, when paired with the number 2300, *evenings* (plural). None of them are metaphoric.  All make perfect sense assuming a plain 24h type morning. More special meanings require a different derived word, not the root word used in Genesis 1.

> **בֹ֖קֶר  ḇō·qer**  = *morning (195), mornings (2), dawn (3), day (1), daybreak (1), every morning (5), soon (1), tomorrow morning (1), end of darkness (?), coming of dawn (?), beginning of day (?), all day (?), in the morning (?), bright joy after night distress (?), next day (?),* could be metaphoric

The root word בֹ֖קֶר occurs only [9x](http://biblehub.com/hebrew/voker_1242.htm) meaning *morning* or *dawn*. None of them are metaphoric.  All make perfect sense assuming a plain 24h type morning.  More special meanings require a different derived word, not the root word used in Genesis 1.

Thus, contrary to Ross, when restricted to the root word, evening and morning do mean parts of a 24h day.

> Evening and morning together in the grammatical structure of Genesis 1 do not appear anywhere else in the Bible. The other contexts they do appear in are too different to imply possibility of the same meaning.

I agree. This is a very important concept! Same words alone are not enough to imply possibility of some meaning. Single words must be in the same word form (root word vs. derived) and same quantity reference (singular vs. plural) and same grammatical context.  Word phrases must in addition be in the some grammatical context for the whole phrase. Hence, there is no phrase *evening and morning* that we can refer to indicating a 24h day.  However, we have already seen that the word עֶ֥רֶב evening on its own as singular root word does indicate a part of a 24h day, and so does בֹ֖קֶר morning as singular root word indicate a part of a 24h day.

> **yom + a number** does mean 24h only 358 out of 359 occurrences outside Genesis. And in all of the singular references its talking about human days, not God's days. And there is not even a grammatical rule that *yom + a number* must mean 24h day.

It is no surprise that there is no grammatical rule for restricting the meaning, since it is not the main purpose of grammatical rules to restrict meaning, just an "optional" side effect.

"All singular days are human days".  Thank you for putting emphasis on singular vs. plural, as indeed all Genesis 1 creation days are in singular form.  This is quite important, since all the extended time period meanings of *yom* come only from the plural use of the word. Further, all of the *yom* in singular + number which do indicate human days are a 24h time period. That's going very clearly with the singular *yom* = 24h day pattern. And finally, it is not even true that they are *all* for human days. In [Exodus 20:10](http://biblehub.com/interlinear/exodus/20-10.htm) we have  day + 7 for humans and then in [Exodus 20:11](http://biblehub.com/interlinear/exodus/20-11.htm) day + 7 for God. Both occurrences of day are in singular. The structure of these two consecutive verses is so similar and the second starting with "for" being an explanation of the first leaves only one reasonable choice: that the length of the human seventh day equals that of God's seventh day. And the remainder of those two verses (plus verse 9) explains the six human workdays (*yom* plural + 6) as modeled by God's six creation days (*yom* plural + 6). Here again it is very clear that both sets of days are of equal duration, since only when they are equally long does it make sense to tell people to copy the pattern. 

Lastly, the 358 out of 359 comment is implying that there is one exception. Ross does not tell us what that exception is. Going by his view that God's seventh day is still continuing I suppose that  the just discussed Exodus 20:11 must be the exception, since it talks about that seventh day of God's rest. Besides the argument above that this instance can not be an exception to the pattern, look at the rest of the sentence: *then He rested on the seventh day*. That implies that the day is over at the time of the author writing Exodus. If the day was still going on the wording should be something like *then He has been and still is resting on the seventh day*. Thus interpreting the seventh day as longer than 24h or even still continuing on till today is not grammatically warranted. (Here we do have the effect that grammatical rules restrict meaning.)

However, comparing again to the [article](http://www.oldearth.org/word_study_yom.htm) similar to this chapter, possibly the $1\over359$ exception to the pattern is "In Zechariah 14:7-9, the "one day" refers to a period of time when the Lord shall be king over the earth." I already showed above that this claim is false and this instance of *one day* is also 24h. But if, for sake of argument, we assume this is indeed the one exception Ross is referring to, then he must be agreeing that in Exodus 20:11 yom + 7 does mean 24h and thus that the last day of creation week was only 24h and is not continuing.  Either way I do not see any reason to believe that there is an exception to the *yom + number = 24h* pattern.

> Theologian Steinman agrees with Basil, but Ross claims " 'And was evening, and was morning, day one' could just as easily suggest a meaning other than 24h."

And why is that? That view does not come easily to me. Especially after reading Basil saying  "in reality a day is the time that the heavens starting from one point take to return there. > Hosea 6:2 contains *yom + number* and it refers to a long period, days, years, 1000 years or even longer

Hosea 6:2 “After *two days* he will revive us; on *the third* day he will restore us, that we may live in his presence”.

The structure of this verse is an X/X+1 poetic synonymous parallelism as in Job 5:19, Prov 6:16, 30:15,18, Amos 1:3.  That means that there is likely some figurative use of words here. However, not of the word *yom*, but of the numbers. The verse communicates that the restoration of Israel mentioned in the previous verse will happen quickly and surely. Thus the days are either real days or short time periods. And it may not be two or three days exactly, but it has to be few days. Otherwise, how can the Hosea author expect that "that we may live before him". So long ages are ruled out here - especially the "1000 years or even longer" that Ross claimed.

And even if the meaning was a long age, which it is not, then still this peculiar verse structure maces it so different from the structure in Genesis 1 that transferring implications on meaning from Hosea 6:2 to Genesis would not be warranted.

So in summary: Hosea 6:2 has figurative elements, but does not mean long ages and does not have a structure that allows transfer to Genesis.

> whichever literal translation of *yom* one chooses, ...

Ross seems to misunderstand what a dictionary entry for a word allows. If a word has four meanings according to the dictionary, then we can still not choose just any of it when the word is used in a larger context such as verse and chapter. The context is what narrows the meaning of the word to possibly just a single one of the dictionary entries. This misunderstanding seems to carry through along all of the chapter as assumption driving the analysis.

However, for the concrete paragraph here it is not so significant, as the main point is about Gen 1:1-2:

> Gen 1:1-2 proclaim the formation of the universe and the earth as predating the events of the six creation days. This is justified by the grammar of Genesis 1:1-2 as it makes clear that the creation of heavens and earth and the being formless of the earth were both completed before the remainder of verse 2 and onward. That implies an unspecified amount of time passed after the creation of the universe and again between the initial created state of the earth before verse 2 continues. Thus the Bible allows both, the universe and the earth to be as old as astronomers and physicists measured it. 

The grammatical justification sounds reasonable. But Ross draws implications and conclusions that are very strong.  William Lane Craig's [analysis](http://www.reasonablefaith.org/defenders-2-podcast/transcript/s8-01) of Genesis 1:1-2 points out that the two verses are connected with *waw* (and) plus a non-predicate and a predicate, which means the clause that precedes the *waw* furnishes background information to what follows. While that further supports the grammatical analysis of Ross, Craig shows that in addition verses 1 and 2 are really one run-on verse because of that *waw* (and) connection. That I think implies that the content of verse 2 is timing wise right after verse 1 - not billions of years later. If the latter was the case, should verse 2 not at least start a new sentence? A little later Craig concludes that verse 1 is global scope and then verse 2 narrows the scope to just what God does with the earth.

Another point of contention is translating *the heavens and the earth* as the entire universe as we know it today including our solar system and all the other stars. My understanding is that  *the heavens and the earth* refers to *all there is* at the time in question. So in Gen 1:1 God made everything there was at the state of Gen 1:2. Now clearly that is the earth itself, and from later verses we can deduce some form of water  containing atmosphere as well.  From Job 38:4-7 we may conclude that angels were shouting for joy as God was making the earth. Hence the creation of the heavens must have included the dwelling place for the angels as well - and the angels in it.  Going even further to also include the sun, moon and stars seems beyond the text, especially since Genesis 1:16 tells us God made them on day four. Therefore the final conclusion that the universe and earth may be billions of years old is not warranted.
