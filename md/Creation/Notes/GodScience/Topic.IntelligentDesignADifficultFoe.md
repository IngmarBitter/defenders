Intelligent design a difficult foe	

For many, evolution theory hard to grasp

May 20, 2006. 01:00 AM

JAY INGRAM

Comments by Ingmar Bitter, PhD,        May 21, 2006	

Scientists are absolutely correct to argue that intelligent design — the claim that a designer, not evolution, created life on Earth — is not science and does not belong in science classrooms. [This is stated as a fact, but is actually just an opinion.] But it might come as a surprise to many of them that simply saying so isn't enough. 

First, to understand why intelligent design isn't science, you do have to know something about what science is. [So here comes the definition/characterization …] Scientists constantly test their theories, trying to poke holes in them. [This is done for ID as well] They perform observations and/or experiments to do that. [This is not done for theoretical physics theories such as quarks, strings, superstrings, negative time, …, those are only accepted as science, because they offer better explanatory power.  ID’s main appeal is also because of its better explanatory power. However, experiments are also done in ID with observing irreducibly complex natural systems.] If their preconceptions are not supported by what they see, detect or calculate, they are discarded. [The real world of science is not that simply black and white. If a theory explains some observations, but not others, it may still remain accepted for very long, it may only be refined what the valid range of applicability is. I.e. Newton mechanics is not applicable for single atoms and not at speeds near the speed of light, but it works great for large slow objects. Similarly, mutations and natural selection work great when confined to the explanation of adaptation of an existing species to new environmental circumstances. ]

Darwin's theory of evolution has been subjected to more than a century's worth of testing. Not once has a fundamental prediction made by the theory been shown to be incorrect. [Here is one: A flagellum rotary motor of 50 proteins, each of which being essential to the functionality, and half of them being unique in the DNA sequence, and no path of arriving at the currently observed state via functional but less complex precursors and thus no way for evolution theory to explain this observable biological engine is proof of Darwin’s theory being incorrect. If you disagree - why? - and what would be the characteristics of an acceptable proof?]

It's true that the story of life on Earth is still incomplete, something that ID proponents (and the creationists before them) have seized on by arguing that, for instance, there are no fossil forms that show transitions from one species to another. But such claims are not true. Transitions between land animals and whales, fish and four-legged terrestrials have been found. There are still gaps, but the point is that there are no new fossils that disprove Darwin. [The gaps are still very large, the fillers in the gaps are objectionable, and what would be the characteristics of a Darwin disproving fossil?  For an early branch in the evolution tree, Darwin would predict very different fossils, but would a pair of very different fossils with a single identical component be accepted as counter example, or would you just argue it away as “this part is evidence for repeated parallel development”. For a late branch in the evolution tree, Darwin would predict very similar fossils, but would a pair of very similar fossils with one large and very distinctly different component be accepted as counter example, or would you just argue it away as “this part is evidence for a branch in the evolution tree when mutations that were selected only occurred in this one component”. So no matter what Fossil is found, Darwinian theorists may never accept any as disproof.]

Proponents of intelligent design, on the other hand, do no experiments. They have promised them in the past, but so far, nothing. Instead, they simply criticize evolution. 

[This is not true.  The observation and study of irreducibly complex system involves many experiments.  To show that a system requires all of its components, one has to genetically engineer the DNA to disable one component at a time and then show that the organism can not use the complete component without it.  In addition, there is further experimental proof for ID by the fact that a host of counter experiments failed. I.e. failure to observe (1) self-creation of DNA from basic ingredients; (2) self-creation of a complete DNA replication system, (3) evolution from single cell to multi cell, (4) evolution from non-breathing to breathing, (5) evolution from non-flying to flying, (6) evolution from self reproduction to dual sex reproduction.] So, they're talking about science but they're not doing any. [Not true, as just explained.  In addition, a lot of the Evolution science is done on highly competitive government grants. If only one in ten grant proposals is funded and Evolution being the theory supported by the grant selection committee, then the chance of ID research being funded is minimal.] Science is about doing something. ID should not be taught in science classes because it isn't science. [Good conclusion, if the premise was correct.]

That all seems pretty straightforward. So why does ID have so much traction in the United States? [Because of the reasons I gave in my comments above.]

Two interesting takes on this have been published in the last few days. One is an article in the journal Public Library of Science Biology about the work of Jon Miller at Northwestern University medical school.

Miller has been measuring scientific literacy over the past 30 years. In the United States and Canada, that literacy is appallingly low. No more than about 15 percent of the general public can read and understand a science article in Time magazine. To his mind, the acceptance of intelligent design is directly related to the strength of the religious right in the U.S.

How about these examples: One out of every three Americans thinks evolution is "definitely false;" only about one out of seven is convinced it's true. [These later ones might very well be part of the 85% that can not read a science article] In a ranking of 34 countries whose adults accept evolution, the United States stands 33rd. (Turkey is 34th.) Perhaps the most extraordinary claim made by Miller is that the United States is the only country in the world where a political party wants ID taught in schools. [This may be a sign that the USA is more willing to embrace new scientific insights than other countries.]

With that background, it should have come as no surprise to scientists that countering the appeal of intelligent design was going to be difficult. [Yes, no surprise here.] Yet, many seemed surprised when that turned out to be the case.

Scott Lilienfeld, a psychologist at Emory University in Atlanta, understands why. In an article in this month's Skeptical Inquirer, Lilienfeld argues that the problem with scientists is that they expect the general public to be sensible about the whole issue and choose evolution. [This is not a vote to choose what one likes, but a matter of scientific competition between two competing hypotheses.]

But should they be? There is, of course, the issue of religion, as I just mentioned. [But not important, it is about science, not religion] But what about those who are on the fence, people who might be churchgoers but are not virulently anti-evolution? Is evolution the "common sense" explanation for the glorious diversity of life? No, it is not. [Because it has serious problems in its approach to generalize from observable small scope adaptation to large scale information enriching evolution from molecules to humans.]

Evolution is hard to grasp. It only makes sense if you're willing to give it millions of years, and if you can grasp the idea that the most infinitesimal changes in genes can, when captured by natural selection, actually create marvellous organs, like the eye, and marvellous species, from fruit flies to blue whales. [I can grasp the idea, but it still involves a lot of hand-waving and even more faith to actually believe the Evolution theory.]

Lilienfeld argues that intuition, so helpful in much of life, is a bad guide to accuracy in this case. The Earth does look flat; it isn't. The sun appears to revolve around the Earth; it doesn't. [Some] Living things appear to have been designed by someone (or Someone); they aren't. [And some appear to possibly have evolved, but not only don’t we know how they got here, they also could not have been evolved through mutations and selection.]

Lilienfeld contends that the solution is to improve the teaching of science in school, to impress upon students that intuition can be wrong, and that the scientific approach is one way of ensuring that doesn't happen. [I agree!]

Of course, if intelligent design is part of that science education, so much for the chances of introducing thinking. [Very high chances! Especially if Evolution and ID are both taught and students have to think about the reasons why they embrace one or the other.]
