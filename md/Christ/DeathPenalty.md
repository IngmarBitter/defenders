# Death as the Penalty for Sin

---

## Ingmar

Romans 6:23 comment by our pastor today: Death here is spiritual death, eternal separation from God. Is that the most common interpretation?

## Grok

Yes, that is one of the most common interpretations among evangelical Protestants and in many standard Bible commentaries.

Romans 6:23 states: "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." In the immediate context of Romans 6 — which discusses freedom from sin's dominion through union with Christ in His death and resurrection — Paul contrasts the outcomes of two ways of life. The pastor's explanation aligns closely with the dominant reading: "death" here primarily denotes spiritual death, understood as separation from God, culminating in eternal separation for those who remain in sin.

**Why This Interpretation Prevails**

- **Contextual contrast:** The verse pairs "death" directly with "eternal life." This parallel suggests "death" is not limited to physical mortality (which all people experience regardless of faith) but refers to the ultimate spiritual consequence — eternal alienation from God's presence and life.
- **Broader biblical usage:** Scripture often portrays sin as causing separation from God (e.g., Isaiah 59:2; Ephesians 2:1, describing people as "dead in your trespasses and sins"). The "second death" in Revelation (20:14-15) reinforces this as eternal judgment.
- **Theological consensus:** Resources from Reformed, Baptist, and general evangelical perspectives (e.g., Ligonier Ministries, GotQuestions.org, and various commentaries) consistently highlight spiritual/eternal death as the core meaning. Physical death is acknowledged as a related consequence of the Fall (Genesis 2-3; Romans 5), but Romans 6:23 emphasizes the deeper spiritual reality.

This view is standard in many pulpits, study Bibles, and sermons, especially in traditions emphasizing personal salvation and the need for regeneration.

**Other Perspectives**

Some interpreters note that "death" (Greek *thanatos*) can encompass multiple dimensions — physical mortality as the immediate result of sin, spiritual separation now, and eternal judgment ultimately. A minority of views (e.g., certain conditional immortality or annihilationist perspectives) argue it refers more literally to cessation of existence rather than ongoing conscious separation, but these are less common in mainstream Protestant exegesis.

## Ingmar

I was thinking of it more as a legal death penalty of physical death. Because then penal substitution via Jesus is easy — He pays our physical death penalty by physically dying. But if Romans 6:23 is spiritual death then how can Jesus' physical death pay for it?

## Grok

Physical death as the straightforward legal penalty does make the substitution seem direct — Jesus dies physically in our place. The common interpretation of "death" here as primarily spiritual death (separation from God) requires explaining how Christ's physical death on the cross addresses that deeper reality.

**Physical Death as Part of the Penalty**

Physical death is indeed a consequence and part of the wages of sin. Paul states in Romans 5:12 that "sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin," referring to the mortality introduced by the Fall. Jesus' physical death on the cross is central to atonement: He was executed as a criminal (though sinless), bearing the outward penalty that the law pronounces on sin. Many sources affirm that Romans 6:23 legitimately includes both physical and spiritual dimensions because sin produces both.

**Why the Verse Emphasizes Spiritual Death**

The immediate context of Romans 6 and the explicit contrast in verse 23 — "the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life" — point to "death" as the opposite of eternal life with God: spiritual death, separation from God now and ultimately eternal separation (the "second death" of Revelation 20). Physical death alone cannot be the primary referent, because believers still die physically yet receive eternal life.

**How Jesus' Physical Death Pays the Full Penalty (Including Spiritual Separation)**

In PSA, the penalty for sin is "death" under God's righteous judgment — comprehensive separation in its physical, relational/spiritual, and eternal aspects. Jesus, as the sinless substitute, bears this penalty in our place. His physical death on the cross is the historical, visible execution of that sentence.

The spiritual dimension is addressed in several interconnected ways:

- **Jesus bears the relational rupture.** On the cross He cries, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34). This expresses the spiritual separation that sin causes. He was "made sin for us" (2 Corinthians 5:21) and bore our iniquities (Isaiah 53:5-6; 1 Peter 2:24), so that we would never have to experience that ultimate abandonment.
- **The efficacy of His person and work.** Because Jesus is the God-man — fully human (so He can die) and fully divine (so His death has infinite value) — one death suffices for the sins of the world. His physical death, offered in perfect obedience as the willing substitute, atones for the spiritual death our sin deserves. The resurrection then publicly vindicates Him and defeats death in all its forms.
- **Union with Christ (the Romans 6 context).** Believers are united to Jesus in His death and resurrection. We are "crucified with Christ" (Galatians 2:20), counted as having paid the penalty in Him. Legally, we are justified; spiritually, we are made alive to God.

In short, Jesus' physical death is the payment of the death penalty. Because of who He is and what He endured (including the spiritual forsakenness on the cross), that single atoning act covers the full wages of sin — physical mortality and the spiritual separation from God. Hence "there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1).

## Ingmar

If 6:23 is both physical and spiritual then it is a one-time physical death and eternal separation. Jesus' sacrifice was a one-time physical death and a temporary spiritual separation. How is that an equivalent substitute?

## Grok

The resolution lies in the infinite value of Christ's person and work, which makes His finite suffering equivalent to — and sufficient for — the eternal penalty finite sinners deserve.

**The Nature of the Penalty and the Substitute**

The penalty (physical death + spiritual/eternal separation) is not merely additive in a mechanical sense for the purpose of substitution. It is the comprehensive judicial sentence under God's holy law and wrath. Jesus, as the sinless God-man (2 Corinthians 5:21; 1 Peter 3:18), bore this sentence in our place:

- **Physically:** He died once on the cross, executing the death penalty in history.
- **Spiritually:** He experienced the Father's forsakenness ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Matthew 27:46), bearing the relational rupture and divine wrath that sin provokes. This was real but temporary, concentrated in His hours on the cross.

The resurrection demonstrates that the payment was accepted and that death was defeated.

**Why This Is Equivalent: The Infinite Dignity of the Substitute**

The worth of the one who suffers determines the value of the atonement. Finite creatures owe an infinite debt because they have sinned against an infinitely holy God; their punishment is therefore eternal. Christ is the eternal Son of God incarnate. His sufferings, though limited in duration, possess infinite merit and value by virtue of His divine person.

As John Piper summarizes a classic formulation: "Though Christ's sufferings were but temporal, yet they were equivalent to our eternal sufferings by reason of the infinite dignity of his person. ... His sufferings equivalent to the eternal sufferings of the whole world." It is not a one-to-one duration match but a matter of qualitative equivalence under divine justice. God accepts the payment because it fully honors His law, satisfies His wrath, and upholds His righteousness (Romans 3:25-26).

**Biblical and Theological Support**

- Scripture emphasizes the once-for-all nature of Christ's sacrifice (Hebrews 9:25-28; 10:10-14), which secures eternal redemption. It does not require perpetual suffering.
- The infinite value of Christ's death is a longstanding confessional affirmation (e.g., Canons of Dort, Second Head, Article 3: "This death of God's Son is the only and entirely complete sacrifice and satisfaction for sins; it is of infinite value and worth").
- For believers, the result is complete: no condemnation (Romans 8:1), reconciliation to God, and the gift of eternal life.

The Substitute's infinite dignity ensures the atonement is not deficient but superabundant. Jesus did not need to endure eternal separation Himself because He exhaustively bore its equivalent in a way only He could.

## Ingmar

So back to the meaning of death. What is better matching to v23: both or only spiritual?

## Grok

For Romans 6:23 specifically, the better and more precise interpretation is that "death" primarily refers to spiritual death — eternal separation from God — rather than both physical and spiritual in equal measure.

**Contextual and Structural Reasons**

- **The immediate contrast:** "the wages of sin is death" against "the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." This parallel strongly suggests "death" is the antithesis of eternal life — ultimate spiritual alienation from God's presence, not merely physical mortality (which believers still experience).
- **Chapter context (Romans 6):** focuses on union with Christ in His death and resurrection, freedom from sin's dominion, and living as "alive to God." Earlier verses (e.g., vv. 16, 21) use "death" in a spiritual/moral sense.
- **Broader Pauline usage:** Paul can speak of physical death as a consequence of sin (Romans 5:12), but in this verse the eschatological and relational dimension predominates.

**Acknowledging Physical Death**

Physical death is undeniably a result of sin in the wider biblical teaching (Genesis 2-3; Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:21-22). Commentators often note Romans 6:23 can legitimately evoke both aspects. However, forcing "both" as equally primary risks diluting the verse's rhetorical force. The primary referent — driven by the eternal-life contrast — is spiritual/eternal death.

In teaching contexts (such as your slides), it is clearest to state: "Death" here primarily means spiritual death — eternal separation from God — though physical death remains a related consequence of sin in the broader biblical narrative.

## Ingmar

So we should see only spiritual death as penalty for sin, and physical death instead as consequence? So Jesus' substitute is needed only for the spiritual death and His physical death was a means to get to the temporary-but-infinite-value spiritual separation?

## Grok

With one important clarification: physical death is both a consequence of the Fall and part of the penal judgment for sin, but Romans 6:23 primarily highlights the spiritual/eternal dimension as the core "wages" in view. Jesus' substitutionary work addresses the full reality, not only the spiritual aspect. His physical death is not merely a vehicle for delivering a temporary spiritual separation; it is the historical execution of the penalty itself, made efficacious by who He is.

**Penalty versus Consequence**

- **Spiritual death** (separation from God, culminating in eternal judgment) is the ultimate penalty emphasized in Romans 6:23 — the direct judicial sentence against sin, the opposite of "eternal life."
- **Physical death** is a real consequence of sin entering the world (Romans 5:12; Genesis 3), but it also functions as part of God's penal order — the visible outworking and sign of the deeper spiritual rupture. Scripture does not sharply separate the two.

**Jesus' Substitutionary Death**

- His **physical death** on the cross is the concrete, once-for-all execution of the death sentence — not secondary or merely instrumental; the means by which the penalty is publicly borne and justice satisfied (Hebrews 9:26-28; Romans 3:25).
- The **spiritual dimension** (separation from the Father) is experienced in concentrated form during the crucifixion — the cry of forsakenness (Matthew 27:46). Real but temporary, because the infinite value of His person carries infinite merit, fully equivalent to the eternal separation sinners deserve.

Thus Jesus does not substitute only for spiritual death. He bears the integrated penalty of death (physical + spiritual) in our place. Possible slide phrasing: "The wages of sin is death — primarily spiritual separation from God, with physical death as its visible consequence and sign. Christ bore both in His substitutionary death, satisfying the full penalty through the infinite worth of His person."

## Ingmar

So where in the Bible does it state that our sins incur the physical death penalty?

## Grok

While Romans 6:23 itself emphasizes the spiritual dimension, the broader biblical teaching presents physical death as part of the penal order resulting from sin.

**Foundational Texts**

- **Genesis 2:17:** "But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die." The first statement connecting disobedience to death; fulfillment includes physical mortality after the Fall (Genesis 3:19; 5:5).
- **Genesis 3:19:** "...till you return to the ground ... for you are dust, and to dust you shall return." Physical death pronounced as part of the judgment for sin.

**Pauline Teaching**

- **Romans 5:12:** "...sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned." The clearest NT statement: sin incurs death (including physical) as a penalty passing to all humanity.
- **Romans 6:23 (wider context):** stands within Romans 5-8, where physical death is already established as part of sin's penalty (Romans 5:12; 8:10-11).
- **1 Corinthians 15:21-22:** "For as by a man came death ... as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive." Physical death is the result of Adam's sin, reversed in Christ's resurrection.

**Other Supporting Passages**

- **Ezekiel 18:4, 20:** "the soul who sins shall die."
- **Hebrews 9:22:** "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins" — the requirement of physical death (blood sacrifice) for atonement.
- **Mosaic Law examples:** capital offenses (Exodus 21:12; Leviticus 20:10) illustrate physical death as the prescribed penalty for specific sins — a shadow of the ultimate penalty.

## Ingmar

"physical death as a penalty or direct consequence of divine judgment" — you are missing the point. I'm not interested in "or". I'm interested in which of the two. The direct consequence is easy. So is there any grounding for "as a penalty for sin as a general rule applicable to us today"?

## Grok

Physical death functions as both a direct consequence of the Fall and a penalty for sin under divine judgment, with strong biblical grounding for the latter as a general rule applicable to humanity today (apart from the redemptive work of Christ).

**Primary Grounding for Physical Death as Penalty**

- **Genesis 2:17:** explicitly a penal threat from God — judicial language of consequence for disobedience. Enacted after the Fall (Genesis 3:19).
- **Romans 5:12-21:** Paul frames death as the "condemnation" and "reign" resulting from sin's guilt — not neutral or merely biological, but the judicial sentence ("judgment," "condemnation") passed on all because of sin (vv. 12, 16, 17). Applies universally today to unregenerate humanity.
- **Romans 6:23 (context):** uses "wages" (earned penalty) language, building on Romans 5.
- **Ezekiel 18:4, 20:** "The soul who sins shall die" — the direct penal sentence for personal guilt.

**Old Testament Legal Framework**

The Mosaic Law repeatedly prescribes physical death as the penalty for various sins (Exodus 21:12 for murder; Leviticus 20:10 for adultery; Deuteronomy 13:6-10 for idolatry). These typify the universal principle that "the wages of sin is death."

**Application Today**

For humanity apart from Christ, physical death remains the operative penalty and consequence of sin's guilt — the outworking of God's righteous judgment on a fallen world (1 Corinthians 15:21-22, 56: "The sting of death is sin"). Believers still die physically, but for them it is transformed — no longer the full penal sentence — because Christ has borne the ultimate penalty.

## Ingmar

How is Genesis 2:17 applicable to us today? There is no tree of life accessible to us. And how is becoming mortal a punishment? We are already mortal.

## Grok

Genesis 2:17 remains directly applicable as the foundational statement of God's covenantal warning and the origin of the penalty of mortality that now governs the human condition. The absence of an accessible Tree of Life does not nullify it; the Fall enacts the penalty permanently for Adam's descendants. Becoming mortal is not circular but a real judicial change imposed as punishment.

**Original Context and Application**

- Adam and Eve were created with the possibility of ongoing life through access to the Tree of Life (Genesis 2:9; 3:22). They were not inherently immortal but dependent on God's provision. The warning is covenantal and judicial.
- The Fall enacts the penalty (Genesis 3): God bars access to the Tree of Life (Genesis 3:22-24), and mortality is imposed ("to dust you shall return," Genesis 3:19). This is a changed condition resulting from divine judgment, not a pre-existing state.

**Applicability Today**

- **Federal headship / imputation:** Romans 5:12-19 — Adam acted as representative for all humanity; "death spread to all men because all sinned." We inherit the guilt and its penal consequences, including physical mortality.
- **No Tree of Life today:** exactly the point of the penalty. The barring of the Tree of Life (Genesis 3:24) made mortality irreversible apart from redemption. Revelation 22:2, 14 restores access only in the new creation for the redeemed.

**Mortality as Punishment**

Humans were not created "already mortal" in the sense of inevitable decay and death as we now experience it. They were created good, with provision for continued life. The punishment consists in the loss of access to the sustaining Tree of Life; the curse of toil, pain, and return to dust (Genesis 3:16-19); and the reign of death as a judicial reality (Romans 5:14, 17, 21). Paul treats this as penal — death is the "condemnation" and "wages" tied to sin's guilt (Romans 5:16-18; 6:23).

## Ingmar

Romans 5:12-21 "for all have sinned" is not universal; it applies only to those past the age of accountability. Infants and the mentally challenged are exempt. But they still die.

But maybe we could say: while the consequence of Adam's sin is that we are born mortal, God could choose to make our physical bodies immortal again if we were to live a morally perfect, holy life, but no one does that, so our mortality is still justified? And the infants and mentally challenged are in a state that is not exactly living life to the fullest, so for them to die and go to heaven is a real improvement?

## Grok

The biblical framework in Romans 5:12-21 and the broader doctrine of original sin treats physical mortality as a universal penalty and consequence of Adam's sin that applies to all humanity, including infants and those with intellectual disabilities. The text does not limit "all have sinned" or the spread of death to those past an age of accountability.

**Romans 5:12-21 and Universality**

- "...death spread to all men because all sinned" (v. 12), developed through federal (covenantal) headship: Adam's one trespass brought condemnation and death to all (vv. 15-19).
- The "all" is universal. **Death reigns even over those who did not sin in the likeness of Adam's transgression (v. 14)** — including infants, who die physically despite never having committed personal, conscious sins. Mortality is the inherited penal consequence of Adam's representative sin, not something limited by individual accountability.
- The "age of accountability" is a pastoral inference (from texts like Deuteronomy 1:39; Jonah 4:11), not explicit in Romans 5, and does not exempt anyone from the physical effects of the Fall.

**Mortality as Inherited Penalty**

Scripture frames mortality as part of the penal judgment on sin (Genesis 2:17; 3:19; Romans 5:16) — not a neutral biological default God might override for someone living perfectly. No one after the Fall lives a morally perfect life (Romans 3:23), so the penalty stands. Even hypothetical perfect obedience would not undo the inherited curse without God's redemptive intervention.

**Infants, the Intellectually Disabled, and Death**

They do die physically, consistent with the universal reign of death through Adam's sin. Evangelical theology (Baptist and Reformed) typically holds that such individuals are safe in Christ through God's grace and the merits of His atonement — not because they are "exempt" from original sin, but because they have not committed willful personal transgressions and are covered by Christ's work.

Your pastoral intuition that "for them to die and go to heaven is a real improvement" is compassionate and aligns with the hope of Scripture (2 Corinthians 5:8: "absent from the body and present with the Lord"). But it is better to attribute their mortality to the corporate effects of the Fall while entrusting their eternal state to God's perfect justice and mercy (Deuteronomy 1:39; Matthew 18:10, 14) — rather than framing their mortality as "justified" by limited earthly experience.

**Summary for Clarity**

- Physical death is the universal penalty and consequence of Adam's sin, applying to all descendants regardless of personal sin or mental capacity (Romans 5:12-14).
- There is no biblical indication that God would grant immortality to anyone living a perfect life post-Fall apart from the redemptive work of Christ.
- For infants and the intellectually disabled, their physical death testifies to the brokenness of creation, while the gospel offers confident hope that they are received by grace into eternal life.
